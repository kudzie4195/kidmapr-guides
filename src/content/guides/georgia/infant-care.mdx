Securing infant daycare in Atlanta is one of the most stressful tasks new parents face — and one of the least intuitive. The parents who navigate it successfully are almost always the ones who started their search before their baby was born. This guide explains why infant slots are so scarce, when to start, and what to do to improve your odds of finding quality care before your return-to-work date.

## Why infant slots are so scarce

The core reason is Georgia's licensing ratio. State law requires licensed childcare centers to maintain a **1:6 staff-to-infant ratio** — one teacher for every six infants. Compare that to the 1:10 or 1:12 ratios allowed in toddler and preschool rooms, and the financial picture becomes clear: infant rooms require more staff per child and generate less revenue per square foot than any other age group. Many centers limit their infant rooms to six to twelve slots total. A few do not accept infants at all.

The result is that Atlanta's supply of licensed infant care is structurally limited. When the overall demand for childcare is high — as it has been throughout metro Atlanta's growth — infant slots become the scarcest resource in the entire childcare market. Waitlists of six to eighteen months are not unusual. In Buckhead, Midtown, and the northern suburbs, eighteen-month waitlists exist at the most sought-after centers.

## When to start your search

Start as soon as you know you are pregnant. This is not an exaggeration. Families who begin their search in the second or third trimester frequently find themselves shut out of their preferred centers entirely, scrambling for whatever has an opening weeks before their maternity or paternity leave ends.

The ideal timeline:

- **First trimester:** Make a list of centers you are interested in. Visit their websites to find out whether they have a waitlist and what it requires.
- **Second trimester:** Tour your top three to five centers and get on their waitlists. Many centers require a non-refundable fee ($50–$200) to join the waitlist. Pay it — these fees are worth it.
- **Third trimester:** Follow up with each center. Ask for your current waitlist position. If a center seems unlikely to have a spot by your return-to-work date, look for additional options.
- **Before birth or shortly after:** Confirm your start date with the center that has offered you a spot, and complete enrollment paperwork as soon as possible.

If you are reading this after your baby is already born, do not give up — just move faster. Start touring and joining waitlists today. Some families do find spots on shorter timelines, especially if their preferred start date is flexible or if they can consider centers slightly outside their neighborhood.

## How to find infant-accepting centers

Not every licensed childcare center accepts infants. Some centers start at 6 weeks, some at 3 months, some at 12 months. The age at which a center begins accepting children is a licensing decision each center makes individually, and it is one of the first things to confirm before putting your name on a waitlist.

Browse [CloverMap](https://clovermap.com) and use the infant care filter to identify licensed centers in your area that accept children under 12 months. You can filter by neighborhood, zip code, and age range to narrow your list quickly. Call before you visit — confirm they currently have or anticipate having infant openings, and ask what their waitlist process looks like.

## Waitlist strategy

Getting on one waitlist is not enough. **Apply to three to five waitlists simultaneously.** There is no penalty for declining an offer from a center after you have already accepted a spot elsewhere — waitlist fees are non-refundable, but accepting one offer does not obligate you to any other center.

When you join a waitlist, ask two questions: What is my current position, and how many infants typically start each month or quarter? A center with twelve infant slots and high staff retention might only open one or two spots per year. A center that is growing or has higher turnover might move through its waitlist much faster.

Stay in touch. Send a brief email every six to eight weeks to confirm you are still interested and to ask for an updated waitlist position. Centers do not always proactively reach out to waitlisted families — a brief, warm check-in can keep you visible without being intrusive.

## Cost overview

Full-time infant care at a licensed Atlanta center typically runs **$1,500 to $2,200 per month**. Costs vary by neighborhood and center type:

- **North Atlanta suburbs (Alpharetta, Sandy Springs, Roswell, Johns Creek):** $1,900–$2,400 per month
- **In-town Atlanta (Midtown, Buckhead, Decatur):** $1,700–$2,200 per month
- **South and West Atlanta:** $1,200–$1,700 per month

Family childcare homes — licensed providers who care for small groups in a residential setting — are often less expensive than centers ($1,000–$1,500 per month) and can offer more flexibility. They are worth including in your search, particularly if a smaller, home-like environment appeals to you.

## CAPS for infant care

If your household meets Georgia's income and work requirements, infant care is fully covered by the **CAPS childcare subsidy**. Georgia's CAPS reimbursement rates for infants are actually **higher than for older children**, reflecting the higher cost of care. See the Georgia CAPS guide for eligibility requirements and how to apply. Confirm that your chosen center is CAPS-approved before you finalize your application — not all licensed centers accept CAPS.

## What to look for in an infant room

When you tour a center, ask to see the infant room specifically. Look for and ask about:

- **Staff ratio and consistency.** Is the ratio 1:6 or better? Does each infant have a primary caregiver assigned to them, or does the staff rotate?
- **Safe sleep practices.** Every infant should be placed on their back in an empty crib. Ask directly about the center's safe sleep policy.
- **Daily communication.** How do teachers communicate with parents about feedings, naps, and diaper changes? Many quality centers use an app that logs updates in real time.
- **Staff tenure.** How long have the infant room teachers been at this center? High turnover in an infant room is a meaningful quality signal — stability and familiarity matter enormously for infants.
- **Feeding support.** If you plan to breastfeed or provide pumped milk, confirm how the center handles breast milk storage, labeling, and preparation.

Trust your instincts during tours. The right center should feel calm, clean, and warm — and the staff should be genuinely happy to answer your questions.
